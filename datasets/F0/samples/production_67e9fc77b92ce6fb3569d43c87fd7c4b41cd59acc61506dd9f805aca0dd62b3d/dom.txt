class="w-dyn-item"><main class="rich-text-glossary w-richtext"><div class="tldr-box">An acceleration clause allows a lender or creditor to declare the entire outstanding balance of a loan, note, or other obligation immediately due and payable upon the occurrence of a specified triggering event, most commonly a payment default. Instead of waiting for payments to come due over the remaining term, the creditor accelerates the maturity of the entire debt. <h2>What Is an Acceleration Clause?</h2>

<p>An acceleration clause is a contractual provision that gives a creditor the right to demand immediate repayment of the entire outstanding balance of a debt upon the borrower's default or the occurrence of other specified events. The clause "accelerates" the maturity date, converting future installment obligations into a single present obligation.</p>

<p>Without an acceleration clause, a lender whose borrower misses a payment can only sue for the missed installment, not for the entire loan balance. The lender must wait for each future installment to come due, then sue for each missed payment individually. This is commercially impractical for a 30-year mortgage or a 10-year term loan. Acceleration solves this problem by making the entire balance due at once, allowing the lender to pursue a single collection action, initiate foreclosure proceedings, or negotiate a restructuring with the full balance as leverage.</p>

<p>Acceleration clauses appear in virtually every credit facility, mortgage, promissory note, bond indenture, and installment sale agreement. They are also used in leases (as acceleration of rent), construction contracts (acceleration of work schedule), and insurance policies (acceleration of premium). The most economically significant application is in secured lending, where acceleration is the precursor to foreclosure or repossession.</p>

<p>The clause distinguishes between automatic acceleration (the entire balance becomes due immediately upon the triggering event without any action by the creditor) and optional acceleration (the creditor has the right but not the obligation to accelerate). Most commercial loan agreements use optional acceleration, which gives the lender flexibility to waive the default, negotiate a forbearance, or accelerate at its discretion.</p>

<h2>Why It Matters</h2>

<p>Acceleration clauses are the enforcement backbone of lending and credit relationships. They convert a manageable installment obligation into an immediate full-balance demand, which fundamentally changes the borrower's risk exposure and the lender's remedial position.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Creditor protection:</strong> Acceleration allows the lender to act decisively when a borrower's creditworthiness deteriorates. Rather than watching the borrower's condition worsen while collecting partial payments, the lender can accelerate and pursue foreclosure, repossession, or workout negotiations with the full balance as leverage. In 2024, accelerated loan balances accounted for approximately 3.5% of U.S. commercial real estate debt, representing approximately $150 billion in outstanding obligations (Mortgage Bankers Association, 2024).</li>
<li><strong>Foreclosure prerequisite:</strong> In real estate finance, acceleration is a legal prerequisite to foreclosure in most jurisdictions. The lender cannot foreclose on the property until it has declared the entire mortgage balance due and payable. The acceleration notice (sometimes called a "demand letter" or "notice of default") triggers the borrower's right to cure and, if uncured, allows the lender to proceed with judicial or non-judicial foreclosure.</li>
<li><strong>Borrower risk:</strong> Acceleration exposes the borrower to the full loan balance as a present obligation. A borrower who misses a $5,000 monthly payment on a $500,000 mortgage faces a $5,000 problem. Upon acceleration, that same borrower faces a $500,000 problem. This asymmetry gives the lender disproportionate leverage and motivates borrowers to cure defaults promptly.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Key Elements of a Well-Drafted Acceleration Clause</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Triggering events:</strong> Define the events that give rise to the right to accelerate. Common triggers include: failure to make scheduled payments, breach of financial covenants (debt service coverage ratio, loan-to-value ratio), breach of non-financial covenants (insurance requirements, reporting obligations), cross-default to other indebtedness, change of control, material adverse change, bankruptcy or insolvency, and judgment liens exceeding a specified threshold.</li>
<li><strong>Notice requirements:</strong> Specify whether the lender must provide notice before acceleration and the content of the notice. Federal and state laws often impose notice requirements (e.g., RESPA for residential mortgages requires a 36-day pre-acceleration notice). Commercial loan agreements typically require written notice specifying the default and, for certain defaults, a cure period.</li>
<li><strong>Cure rights:</strong> Define the borrower's right to cure the default before acceleration takes effect. Cure periods for monetary defaults are typically 5-10 days. Non-monetary defaults may have 30-60 day cure periods. Some agreements distinguish between defaults that can be cured (late payment) and those that cannot (bankruptcy filing, material misrepresentation).</li>
<li><strong>Automatic vs. optional acceleration:</strong> Specify whether acceleration is automatic upon the triggering event or at the lender's election. Automatic acceleration is typically limited to bankruptcy events (to preserve the lender's rights in bankruptcy proceedings). Optional acceleration for all other defaults gives the lender flexibility to pursue forbearance or workout alternatives.</li>
<li><strong>Cross-default and cross-acceleration:</strong> Define whether a default under a related agreement (another loan, a swap agreement, a guaranty) triggers acceleration of the current obligation. Cross-default clauses amplify the consequences of a single default across the borrower's entire debt structure. Include materiality thresholds to prevent minor defaults from triggering cross-acceleration.</li>
<li><strong>Reinstatement and de-acceleration:</strong> Address whether the borrower may reinstate the original payment schedule after acceleration by curing the default and paying all accrued amounts. State foreclosure laws in many jurisdictions provide a statutory right of reinstatement for residential mortgages. Commercial agreements typically do not include reinstatement rights unless negotiated.</li>
<li><strong>Post-acceleration interest and fees:</strong> Specify the interest rate that applies to the accelerated balance (typically the default rate, which is 2-5% above the contract rate), and any additional fees (late charges, acceleration fees, prepayment premiums). Ensure the default rate and fees comply with applicable usury laws.</li>
<li><strong>Effect on guarantors:</strong> Address whether acceleration of the primary obligation automatically triggers the guarantor's liability. Most guaranty agreements provide that acceleration of the guaranteed obligation makes the guarantor immediately liable for the full accelerated amount.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Market Position &amp; Benchmarks</h2>

<h3>Where Does Your Clause Fall?</h3>

<ul class="tier-list">
<li><strong>Lender-Favorable:</strong> Broad triggering events including material adverse change, automatic acceleration for bankruptcy, 5-day cure for monetary defaults with no cure for non-monetary defaults, cross-default to all other indebtedness exceeding $10,000, no reinstatement rights, default rate of contract rate plus 5%, lender recovers all enforcement costs.</li>
<li><strong>Market Standard:</strong> Payment default with 10-day cure, covenant default with 30-day cure, automatic acceleration only for bankruptcy, optional acceleration for all other defaults, cross-default to material indebtedness (exceeding $500,000), borrower may reinstate if default cured within 30 days of acceleration notice, default rate of contract rate plus 2%, reasonable attorney's fees recoverable.</li>
<li><strong>Borrower-Favorable:</strong> Acceleration only for payment default (not covenant defaults), 30-day cure for monetary defaults, no cross-default provisions, borrower may reinstate at any time before foreclosure sale by curing default and paying costs, no default rate increase on accelerated balance, acceleration requires written notice with detailed description of default, lender must accept cure if offered within 60 days.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Market Data</h3>

<ul class="market-data-list">
<li>Acceleration clauses appear in virtually 100% of commercial loan agreements, mortgages, and bond indentures (LMA/LSTA survey, 2024).</li>
<li>The most common cure period for monetary defaults in commercial loans is 5-10 days, with 10 days being the market standard in approximately 55% of syndicated loan facilities.</li>
<li>Cross-default provisions appear in approximately 90% of syndicated loan agreements, with materiality thresholds averaging $25 million for investment-grade borrowers and $5 million for non-investment-grade borrowers.</li>
<li>Automatic acceleration for bankruptcy events is included in approximately 95% of commercial loan agreements. However, the Bankruptcy Code's automatic stay (Section 362) prevents enforcement of acceleration upon the filing of a bankruptcy petition.</li>
<li>Default interest rate spreads of 2% above the contract rate appear in approximately 65% of commercial loan agreements. Spreads of 3-5% are common in mezzanine and high-yield facilities.</li>
<li>Reinstatement rights after acceleration are included in approximately 30% of commercial mortgage agreements and are required by statute in approximately 35 U.S. states for residential mortgages.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common Contract Types</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Commercial loan agreements:</strong> Acceleration is the primary remedial mechanism, triggering the lender's right to pursue collection, foreclosure, or workout negotiations.</li>
<li><strong>Residential and commercial mortgages:</strong> Acceleration is the legal prerequisite to foreclosure proceedings in most jurisdictions.</li>
<li><strong>Promissory notes:</strong> Standard acceleration language allows the holder to demand full repayment upon default.</li>
<li><strong>Bond indentures:</strong> Acceleration requires action by the trustee, typically at the direction of a specified percentage of bondholders.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment financing and leases:</strong> Acceleration of remaining payments upon default, with the equipment subject to repossession.</li>
<li><strong>Installment sale agreements:</strong> Acceleration of the remaining purchase price upon buyer's default, with the seller retaining a security interest in the sold property.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Negotiation Playbook</h2>

<h3>Key Drafting Notes</h3>

<ul class="drafting-notes">
<li>Negotiate meaningful cure periods for curable defaults. The purpose of a cure period is to give the borrower a reasonable opportunity to remedy the default before facing the full consequences of acceleration. A 5-day cure for a financial covenant default that requires a capital infusion is not meaningful. Match cure periods to the time required to actually cure each type of default.</li>
<li>Include materiality thresholds in cross-default provisions. Without thresholds, a $500 unpaid trade debt could trigger cross-default on a $50 million credit facility. Market standard thresholds range from $1 million to $50 million depending on the borrower's size and credit profile.</li>
<li>Address the effect of acceleration on interest rate hedging. If the borrower has entered into interest rate swaps tied to the loan, acceleration may trigger early termination of the swap, resulting in a swap breakage fee. The acceleration clause should address the coordination between loan acceleration and swap termination.</li>
<li>Consider whether acceleration should be mandatory or discretionary for different default types. Automatic acceleration for bankruptcy events protects the lender's claim in bankruptcy. Optional acceleration for other defaults gives the lender flexibility. A lender that automatically accelerates on a technical covenant default may inadvertently push a performing borrower into distress.</li>
<li>Include a waiver of demand, presentment, and protest. Common law requirements for demand and presentment can delay the effectiveness of acceleration. Borrowers routinely waive these requirements, but the waiver must be express.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Common Pitfalls</h3>

<ul class="common-pitfalls">
<li>Accelerating without proper notice. Failure to provide the contractually required notice of default and acceleration can invalidate the acceleration and any subsequent foreclosure. Review the notice requirements carefully before accelerating, including any statutory notice requirements (RESPA Section 6 for residential mortgages).</li>
<li>Triggering unintended cross-defaults. Accelerating one loan may trigger cross-default provisions in the borrower's other credit facilities, creating a cascade of defaults and accelerations that pushes the borrower into insolvency. Lenders should consider the broader implications before accelerating.</li>
<li>Waiving the right to accelerate through inconsistent conduct. A lender that repeatedly accepts late payments without acceleration may be deemed to have waived the right to accelerate for future late payments. The loan agreement should include a no-waiver clause, and the lender should send reservation-of-rights notices when accepting late payments.</li>
<li>Failing to account for the automatic stay in bankruptcy. Upon the filing of a bankruptcy petition, the automatic stay (11 U.S.C. Section 362) prevents the lender from enforcing acceleration, commencing foreclosure, or taking any collection action. Ipso facto clauses (provisions that accelerate based on bankruptcy filing) are unenforceable under Section 365(e) for executory contracts and unexpired leases.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Jurisdiction Notes</h2>

<p><strong>United States:</strong> Acceleration clauses are governed by state contract law, the UCC (for personal property secured transactions), and federal regulations (for residential mortgages). The Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act preempts state restrictions on due-on-sale acceleration in residential mortgages. State foreclosure laws determine the procedural requirements for post-acceleration foreclosure (judicial vs. non-judicial, redemption periods, deficiency judgment availability). Approximately 22 states require judicial foreclosure; the remainder allow non-judicial foreclosure. The CFPB's Regulation X (12 C.F.R. Section 1024.41) requires mortgage servicers to evaluate loss mitigation options before accelerating residential mortgages.</p>

<p><strong>United Kingdom:</strong> English law enforces acceleration clauses in loan agreements as written. The Consumer Credit Act 1974 (as amended) requires lenders to issue a default notice giving the borrower at least 14 days to cure before accelerating consumer credit agreements. The Financial Conduct Authority's Mortgage Conduct of Business rules (MCOB) require lenders to treat residential mortgage borrowers fairly and explore alternatives before acceleration and repossession. English courts have held that default interest rates and fees triggered by acceleration must not be penal (Cavendish Square Holding BV v Talal El Makdessi, 2015).</p>

<p><strong>European Union:</strong> The EU Mortgage Credit Directive (2014/17/EU) requires member states to adopt measures encouraging lenders to exercise reasonable forbearance before initiating enforcement proceedings on residential mortgages. The Consumer Credit Directive (2008/48/EC) provides consumer protections applicable to acceleration of consumer loans. National implementations vary: France's Code de la Consommation provides for detailed pre-acceleration notice requirements and mandatory mediation. Germany's BGB Section 498 requires two consecutive missed payments and a two-week cure period before acceleration of consumer installment loans.</p>
